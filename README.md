## The Number Behind the Headline

Precision matters here. The 25 lakh figure quoted in Parliament and ministry briefings describes India’s entire electronics manufacturing base over ten years, direct and indirect jobs combined, and it rests on industry estimates. The large-scale electronics PLI scheme itself reports a little over a lakh of direct jobs, while approved Semicon India projects are projected to create around 25,000 advanced-technology direct jobs and 60,000 indirect ones.

That distinction is useful. Mobile assembly absorbed enormous volumes of operators, but chip fabs, packaging units and design houses ask for a thinner, more demanding slice of talent. Anyone advising HR heads should know which slice they are actually discussing, because inflated numbers erode trust fast.

## Where the Real Hiring Pressure Sits

Look at the job descriptions coming out of new facilities. Process integration engineers, equipment engineers, yield and reliability specialists, test engineers, facilities experts for ultra-pure water and specialty gases, and technicians who respect cleanroom discipline every single shift. Graduates are plentiful, but people who have worked inside a running fab are not, and that experience cannot be shortcut.

Scarcity breeds poaching. When a handful of projects ramp up in the same year, a good yield engineer receives several calls a month, counter-offers become routine, and attrition quietly inflates every project budget. Employers who rely only on portals tend to meet the same ten candidates as their competitors.

## A Practical Plan for the Next Two Quarters

Start by mapping roles into two groups: positions where experience is non-negotiable, and positions where structured training works. Hire seasoned people for process ownership and yield, and build onboarding for operators and test technicians from diploma and engineering pipelines. Share requirements early, benchmark pay against actual offers rather than last year’s survey, and keep decision cycles short, because strong candidates rarely wait beyond a week.

Quick deployment is realistic when a consultancy already holds vetted talent, and the client answers feedback within days. It is unrealistic for rare fab-experienced profiles, and an honest partner will tell you that on the first call.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1. Does the 25 lakh figure apply to chip fabs directly?

No. The figure covers India’s whole electronics manufacturing sector over a decade, direct and indirect combined. Semiconductor projects add a narrower layer of highly specialised roles, which is exactly where hiring is hardest. Treat 25 lakh as the market backdrop, not as a count of chip jobs waiting to be filled.

### Q2. Which semiconductor roles are hardest to fill?

Process integration, equipment and yield engineers top the list, followed by test and reliability engineers and cleanroom-experienced technicians. Fab experience takes years to build, so supply stays thin even when engineering graduates are plentiful, and employers often end up competing for the same small group of seasoned professionals.

### Q3. Can a consultancy deliver chip talent quickly?

Yes, when it already holds a verified, pre-screened database. Shortlists then move in days, while assignment tests protect quality. Roles needing rare fab experience still take longer, and a good partner says so upfront instead of promising a timeline the market cannot support.

### Q4. Should employers hire experienced people or train freshers?

Do both, by role. Hire experienced people for process and yield ownership, where mistakes are costly, and train diploma holders and graduates for operator and test positions, where structured onboarding closes the gap within months and keeps long-term costs under control.
